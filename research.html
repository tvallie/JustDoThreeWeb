Research

The science checks out.

The idea behind Just Do Three isn't just intuition — research in psychology, neuroscience, and productivity consistently points in the same direction.

Trying to do too many things at once makes it harder to finish meaningful work. Research shows that multitasking and constant task switching reduce focus, increase mistakes, and slow progress. Instead of true multitasking, the brain rapidly switches between tasks — and every switch carries a cognitive cost.

Below are a few articles and studies that explore why focusing on fewer priorities leads to better results.

Landmark Research on Focus, Multitasking, and Task Backlogs

The Hidden Productivity Cost of Multitasking

American Psychological Association · apa.org

Psychological research shows that what people call multitasking is usually rapid task switching. Each switch creates small mental delays as the brain disengages from one task and re-engages with another. These delays accumulate and can significantly reduce productivity and focus over time.

The Science of Single-Tasking and Focus

The Science Behind Single-Tasking and Focus

mindspacex.com

Research on attention and cognitive load shows that focusing on one task at a time improves efficiency, accuracy, and satisfaction. Dividing attention across many tasks creates mental overload and reduces the quality of work. Single-tasking allows the brain to dedicate full cognitive resources to the problem in front of it.

The Power of Single-Tasking

psychologytoday.com

Psychologists explain that what we often call multitasking is actually rapid attention switching, not true parallel processing. Single-tasking improves performance by eliminating the cognitive costs of constantly shifting focus between tasks. When attention remains on one task, deeper thinking and better outcomes are possible.

The "Switch Cost" of Multitasking

news.wfu.edu

Researchers studying attention describe a hidden productivity penalty called the switch cost. Each time the brain moves from one task to another it must disengage from the previous task and re-engage with the new one, which slows performance and reduces efficiency.

Multitasking and Productivity Loss

apa.org — American Psychological Association

Research summarized by the American Psychological Association shows that even brief mental blocks caused by switching between tasks can consume a large portion of productive time. Studies have found that frequent task switching may cost up to 40% of a person's productive time due to the cognitive effort required to re-orient to each task.

Why Multitasking Feels Productive but Isn't

Studies also show that most people believe they are good at multitasking even though performance declines when switching between complex tasks. In fact, only a very small percentage of people can perform two complex tasks simultaneously without measurable performance loss.

What this means

Across many studies, the conclusion is consistent:

  • Multitasking is usually rapid task switching
  • Every switch creates a cognitive cost
  • Focusing on fewer tasks improves completion and quality

Less switching. Less overwhelm. More progress.
